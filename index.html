Hello! I'm Andrew, originally from Singapore but can now be found at Champaign, Illinois (or sometimes hiking and exploring one of the 63 national parks).

I do accounting research, but like many of us out there, I don't know exactly what it entails since it's so broad (once heard a dude present a working paper on tariff's impact on tourism? I guess it falls under accounting as well?). What I do know is that I'm interested in how information moves through financial markets, how it gets created, disclosed, interpreted, and sometimes misunderstood.

I'm particularly drawn to the tension between theory and reality. Ball and Brown (1968) showed that when companies announce their earnings, stock prices actually respond, a simple finding that opened up the entire empirical accounting literature. Then with Fama's (1970) Efficient Markets Hypothesis came the arguments that it is not always true, as Bernard and Thomas (1989) suggested that prices continue to drift in the direction of earnings surprises for months after the announcement (so the market is inefficient and we can profit from it?). Sloan (1996) showed that investors focus on earnings but ignore whether those earnings come from actual cash or from accounting adjustments, and they get it wrong in predictable ways. It fascinates me that investors are human (they have limited attention, they anchor on certain numbers, they make predictable mistakes), and that the way information is packaged matters just as much as the information itself.

The theoretical literature on disclosure also explores the behaviours between managers and investors. I find myself wondering about the strategic dance between the two sides. Managers know more about their company than investors do, and they decide what to reveal, what to hide, and how to frame it. Investors know that managers are strategic, so they don't take everything at face value, they read between the lines and try to fill in the gaps. Grossman (1981) and Milgrom (1981) show us the unraveling effect, where, if there's no cost to disclosing information, managers end up revealing everything because investors will assume the worst if they don't. But when disclosure has costs, Verrecchia (1983) models how managers disclose some things and hide others. And Dye (1985) and Jung and Kwon (1988) explore what happens when the market can't function properly because companies don't fully know their true quality and investors can't fully infer what's being hidden. Beyond these disclosure models, I'm also drawn to Spence's (1973) signaling framework, which uses education and talent as examples of costly signals. It's a really smart way to think about voluntary disclosure, and it highlights how costly actions can serve as credible signals of private information. I'm interested in what these models get right about real managerial behaviour, but I'm equally curious about where they fall short, especially when we start introducing new technologies that might change the very nature of how information is created and consumed.

Lately, I've been drawn to AI and how it might reshape all of this. Blankespoor, deHaan, and Li (2026) examine how AI is changing the disclosure landscape, not just what gets disclosed, but how it's framed, written, and read. I find myself wondering about the two sides of this. On one hand, managers might use AI to generate financial text, choosing tones and frames that they think best serve their reporting objectives. On the other hand, analysts and investors might use AI to read and interpret those same disclosures, extracting patterns that humans might miss or process faster than we ever could. But what happens when AI-generated text meets AI-powered reading? Does the intended message get through, or does it get distorted? Does AI pick up on the same cues as a human analyst? And if both sides are using AI, does the information flow become more efficient, or does it become a conversation where humans are no longer in the loop?

I should say, I'm still early in my PhD, and my views will almost certainly change (tbh, I hope they do, because if I'm thinking the same things five years from now, something's gone wrong). I don't have all the answers, and I don't expect to. But I enjoy thinking about these things, and I'm excited to see where the data takes me.
